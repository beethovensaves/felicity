# Final Anti-Slop Pass

Use this after the draft has passed the meaning, evidence, audience, and structure checks. Read the
whole piece once for its rhythm and its last line. Then inspect the moves below. A phrase is not a
defect merely because it matches an example. Change it when it delays the point, implies more than
the evidence supports, obscures an actor or relation, or replaces the author's voice with a stock
performance. Preserve useful humor, idiom, fragments, technical terms, and deliberate emphasis.

## Delayed or staged point

- Throat-clearing: "Here's the thing," "Let me be clear," "In today's world."
- Faux insight: "What nobody tells you," "The part everyone misses."
- Rhetorical setup: "What if I told you," "Question? Answer," "Plot twist."
- Dramatic colon reveal: "The secret: it learns." Keep colons that actually introduce a list,
  explanation, label, or quotation.

Cut the staging and state the supported point. Keep an opening story or aside when it supplies
context, tension, or character.

## Inflated or unsupported meaning

- Importance puffery: "a pivotal moment," "a testament to," "underscores its significance."
- Interpretive commentary: "That last part matters," "As you can see," redundant "In other
  words." Let the facts or explanation carry the weight.
- Superficial analysis: trailing "highlighting," "showcasing," or "reflecting" clauses that
  announce a virtue instead of explaining a consequence.
- Weasel authority: "experts agree," "studies show" without a source this reader can assess.
- Fake-strong verbs: "serves as a hub," "plays a role" when "is," "has," or a concrete action is
  clearer.

Replace an inflated line with a fact already in the source, narrow the claim, or remove it. Never
invent a metric, example, motive, source, or causal link to make the sentence sound concrete.
When a sentence consists only of vague praise, a fluent synonym is not a useful rewrite. State what
concrete function or result the writer needs to supply.

## Mechanical shape and closure

- Binary contrast or negative listing: "It's not X. It's Y," "Not this. Not that. This."
- Synonym cycling: new labels for one object used only to avoid repetition.
- Dramatic fragmentation, repeated sentence shapes, padded triads, or a run of similarly short
  paragraphs that create robotic rhythm.
- Decorative em dashes, emoji headings, bold emphasis, bullets better read as prose, or headings
  over tiny sections.
- A final aphorism, grand prediction, or recap that repeats the piece instead of ending on its last
  useful point or next action.

Use the plain structure that fits the relationship between ideas. Keep a genuine contrast, fragment,
list, or em dash when it improves the reader's understanding or fits the author. A sentence that
could move unchanged to many other subjects probably needs a specific mechanism or should be cut.

## Last check

Compare the result with the source. The claims, degree of certainty, commitments, and distinctive
voice must still belong to the author. If the edit has made every paragraph equally tidy, restore
the useful variation.
